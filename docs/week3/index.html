What I learned

Describe your design: why these routers, LANs and links, and how you chose the addresses.

UI used 4 routers in a ring (r1-r2-r3-r4-r1), each with one LAN and one host. The ring means every router has two ways to reach any other router. I picked the LAN addresses in pairs so I could summarise them: 10.41.10.0/24 and 10.41.11.0/24 on the top, 10.41.20.0/24 and 10.41.21.0/24 on the bottom. The links are /30s, named after the routers they connect (12, 23, 34, 14). Each router has a route to its neighbour's LAN, one /23 for the other side, and routes to the two links it isn't connected to. I added those link routes after pingall showed routers couldn't reply to each other.

Which route summarises several LANs? Which networks does it cover, why is it correct, and what else would it attract if you added a network inside its range?

r1 and r2 use 10.41.20.0/23 to reach both h3 and h4, and r3 and r4 use 10.41.10.0/23 for h1 and h2. 10.41.20.0/23 covers 10.41.20.0 to 10.41.21.255. The third octets, 20 and 21, only differ in the last bit, so 23 bits are shared. 20 is even, so the /23 covers exactly these two LANs and nothing more.

When you took a link down, what happened, and why couldn't your routes recover even though another path existed? What would have recovered?

I took down the r1-r4 link. The traceroute from h1 to h3 stopped at r1 with !N, and ip route get said "Network is unreachable". Linux deleted r1's routes that used that link, including the /23. The other path through r2 and r3 was still there, but static routes can't use it. A routing protocol like OSPF would notice and switch to r2 and r3 by itself. A backup static route with a shorter prefix, like 10.41.0.0/16 via r2, would also work, the same way the /20 caught traffic in the lab.

In your network, what was the control plane? Who worked out the routes, and what does that mean for a network of 40 routers?

I was the control plane. I worked out every route myself and wrote it in network.yaml. check_paths() checked for loops and missing routes, and build_routed.py put the routes on the routers. That's fine for 4 routers, but not for 40. Every router would need a route to every network, and every change or failure would need a manual fix. A real network needs a routing protocol like OSPF, or an SDN controller, to work out routes automatically. Keeping the network as data and checking it first is still worth doing at that size.
